## Novelty statement (draft v1, 2026-10-21, see D-012)

The loss itself is the SDTP/DTP target: with `G` approximating an inverse, the target for a middle state is `h + G(label) - G(prediction)` (Lee et al. 2015, Eq. 15; Bartunov et al. 2018, SDTP). We do **not** claim a new loss. Our contribution is a compute-matched empirical study of using that target as an *auxiliary* signal next to ordinary backpropagation: FLOPs-to-target against tuned baselines (with learning-rate schedules and a re-tuning noise floor, per Schmidt et al. 2021), controls for a lower-layer learning-rate effect and for DSN-style deep supervision and autoencoder auxiliaries, and a mechanism diagnosis through gradient alignment. Prior work evaluated TP-style targets as *replacements* for backpropagation and judged final accuracy, without counting compute.

| Lee, Zhang, Fischer, Bengio, "Difference Target Propagation" | arXiv:1412.7525; ECML PKDD 2015 | Replaces gradients with layer-wise targets. Each layer has a learned inverse `g_i` trained with a noise-injected reconstruction loss. DTP target: `h_{i-1} + g_i(target_i) - g_i(h_i)`. The top hidden layer's target uses the true gradient. Theorem 1: with perfect inverses and a small step, the TP update is within 90 degrees of the BP update (a lower bound on cos of the angle). Theorem 2: the difference correction makes the next layer's output move toward its target under a Jacobian condition. Results: MNIST 7x240 tanh 1.94% vs BP 1.86% error; with ReLU 3.15% vs 1.62%; CIFAR-10 fully connected 50.71% vs 53.72% accuracy. | Our target is this formula at one middle layer. Its theory motivates our gradient-alignment diagnostic (H3). Its warnings: ReLU hurts TP; the inverse is hard to learn when the output layer is much smaller than the layer below. | We keep backprop and use the target as an auxiliary loss; `G` inverts the whole top half; we measure compute to a target. |
| Bartunov, Santoro, Richards, Hinton, Lillicrap, "Assessing the Scalability of Biologically-Motivated Deep Learning Algorithms and Architectures" | arXiv:1807.04587; NeurIPS 2018 | Tests DTP, a gradient-free simplified DTP (SDTP), SDTP with auxiliary outputs (AO-SDTP), feedback alignment and direct feedback alignment on MNIST, CIFAR-10 and ImageNet. SDTP target for the penultimate layer: `h - g(h_out) + g(correct label)`. Results (fully connected, test error): MNIST BP 1.48%, DTP-alternating 1.83%, SDTP-alternating 2.28%; CIFAR BP 41.32%, DTP 42.32%, SDTP 54.27%, AO-SDTP 45.40%. ImageNet top-1 error: BP 71.79%, DTP 98.34%, SDTP 99.28%. Appendix 5.6: backprop is a special case of TP with the Jacobian transpose as the "inverse"; proposes hybrid targets (convex mix of the two) as unexplored. | **The SDTP target is identical to ours.** The low-entropy label target is the weakness they diagnose (our risk R-09). Alternating training of forward and inverse was more stable than parallel. | Used next to BP, not as a replacement; compute-matched evaluation; controls; we adopt their AO idea as variant V2. |
| Lee, Xie, Gallagher, Zhang, Tu, "Deeply-Supervised Nets" | arXiv:1409.5185; AISTATS 2015 | Companion classifier objectives on hidden layers, trained together with the output loss by backprop; hinge threshold and a decaying weight make them fade out. Claims faster convergence (loose theory), better generalization, larger gains with small data (26% at 500 samples), 4.55 times larger first-layer gradient variance. See `docs/notes/dsn-reference-notes.md`. | Arm B baseline: hidden-layer auxiliary losses with backprop. | Our auxiliary target comes from a learned inverse, not directly from the label; compute is counted; tuned baselines. |
| Rasmus, Valpola, Honkala, Berglund, Raiko, "Semi-Supervised Learning with Ladder Networks" | arXiv:1507.02672; NeurIPS 2015 | Denoising targets at every layer with lateral skip connections, trained jointly with the supervised loss by backprop. Normalization is needed to avoid the trivial constant solution. Permutation-invariant MNIST: 100 labels 1.06% vs 21.74% baseline; all labels 0.57% vs 0.80%. A plain decoder-autoencoder auxiliary gave no significant gain over the supervised model. Training compute roughly triples. | Local auxiliary targets plus backprop; the collapse warning (our risk R-03); plain autoencoder auxiliaries may not help (arm C). | Labels, not unsupervised denoising, drive our target; compute-matched comparison. Their gains were largest in the low-label regime, which suggests adding a low-label experiment. |
| Schmidt, Schneider, Hennig, "Descending through a Crowded Valley: Benchmarking Deep Learning Optimizers" | arXiv:2007.01547; ICML 2021 | Benchmarks 15 optimizers on 8 DeepOBS problems (TensorFlow 1.15) with budgets of 1, 25, 50 and 75 random-search runs, four schedules, and 10 seeds for the best setting. No clear winner; tuning helps about as much as trying other optimizers; schedules add about 5% median; re-tuning the same optimizer shifts results about as much as switching optimizers. | Not an overlap: our evaluation methodology. | We adopt: 25-trial budgets, schedules, 10 final seeds, re-tuning noise floor (D-014). We do not claim comparison with their published numbers unless we run the same DeepOBS tasks and protocol. |

| Jiang, Wong, Zhou, Andersen, Dean, Ganger, Joshi, Kaminsky, Kozuch, Lipton, Pillai, "Accelerating Deep Learning by Focusing on the Biggest Losers" (Selective-Backprop) | arXiv:1910.00762 (2019); read in full | Uses the forward pass to decide whether an example is used for the backward pass: selection probability is the percentile of the loss among recent losses raised to a power beta (selectivity). Selected examples are collected until a full training batch exists, so more examples are forwarded than updated on. Reports up to 3.5x faster to target error than standard SGD on CIFAR10, CIFAR100 and SVHN (wall-clock, image models, GPU), 1.02x to 1.8x faster than an importance-sampling method, and 26% more with stale forward passes. Shows that gradients from only high-loss examples keep a higher cosine similarity with the full gradient than random ones (agrees with our E-009). Working selectivity range reported as 20% to 65% (verify exact values per dataset) | Same idea (skip backward on low-loss examples); the check of gradient concentration | Our S keeps the top-k rows of the batch it already ran forward: no extra selection passes, a smaller backward batch, no loss history. Our evaluation counts FLOPs exactly, against several tuned baselines, with paired seeds, a batch-size control and a random-row control. Their cost per update is higher than a plain update in our counter (`docs/notes/improve-reference-arms.md`) |
| Kawaguchi and Lu, "Ordered SGD: A New Stochastic Optimization Framework for Empirical Risk Minimization" | arXiv:1907.04371; AISTATS 2020; read in full | Per minibatch of size s, takes the top-q examples by loss and updates with the average gradient over them (one line: mean of `torch.topk(loss, q)`). Proves that this minimizes an ordered empirical loss L_q, convergence rates and a generalization bound. Default rule for q: q = s at the start, then s/2, s/4, s/8, s/16 when the training accuracy passes 80%, 90%, 95%, 99.5%, updated at the end of each epoch. Test error improves on 18 problems (MNIST LeNet 0.65% to 0.57%, CIFAR10 PreActResNet18 13.75% to 12.87%, no augmentation). Wall-clock per epoch: no saving on MNIST LeNet (14.44 s against 14.77 s), 4% to 7% saving on PreActResNet18 | **The rule of our arm S** (top-k of a minibatch, gradient of their mean) **and a kept fraction that shrinks during training.** Their stated goal is generalization (a different objective), not compute; they note that importance sampling aims to speed up minimizing the average loss | Our goal is FLOPs-to-target on the average loss, with the backward pass sliced to the kept rows so the saving is real and exactly counted (my reading, not tested: in the one-line implementation autograd still runs the backward pass over the whole batch, with zero gradient for the rows not kept; the paper reports only small per-epoch time savings); our gradient is scaled by 1/B, theirs by 1/q; we test the shrinking-q schedule under our protocol rather than assume it |
| Katharopoulos and Fleuret, "Not All Samples Are Created Equal: Deep Learning with Importance Sampling" | arXiv:1803.00942; ICML 2018; read in full | Pre-samples a large batch B, scores each example with an upper bound on its gradient norm (the norm of the loss gradient at the last layer's pre-activations, computed in the same forward pass), resamples a batch b with replacement and re-weights (unbiased). Turns importance sampling on only when an estimate of the variance reduction exceeds a threshold. Reports lower training loss and 5% to 17% lower test error for equal wall-clock on CIFAR, fine-tuning and an LSTM. Finds that sampling by loss increases variance, especially early in training, and helps only when most losses are near zero. Names reducing the number of examples in the backward pass as future work | Selection of informative examples from a larger batch; evidence that loss is a weaker score than the gradient-norm bound early in training; the idea of switching selection on when it pays | Our rule is biased by design (rows are dropped, not re-weighted) and uses the loss; the gradient-norm bound is a candidate score for S (free to compute); our warm-up is a fixed stand-in for their switch |
| Loshchilov and Hutter, "Online Batch Selection for Faster Training of Neural Networks" | arXiv:1511.06343; ICLR 2016 workshop; method and setup read, results not checked in detail | Ranks all training points by their latest known loss and samples a batch with probability decaying exponentially with rank; the selection pressure (ratio of largest to smallest probability) is **decreased** exponentially over epochs; losses are refreshed periodically. MNIST (50,000 / 10,000 / 10,000), batch size 64, batch normalization, AdaDelta and Adam; abstract reports about 5x speed-up. Selection overhead about 5% per step, about 20% with loss recomputation | Same dataset as ours; loss-based selection; a schedule of the selection pressure over training (opposite direction to Ordered SGD's shrinking q) | Their gain is in the number of epochs or time with a full backward pass on the sampled batch; ours skips backward rows and counts FLOPs; the two schedule directions disagree, so we test both rather than assume one |
| Shrivastava, Gupta, Girshick, "Training Region-based Object Detectors with Online Hard Example Mining" (OHEM) | arXiv:1604.03540; CVPR 2016; read in full | Forward all candidate regions of an image, sort by loss (with non-maximum suppression), update on the B hardest; implemented with a read-only copy of the network so that backward runs only for the selected regions, because masking the loss still pays the backward pass in the toolboxes of that time. Aim: accuracy and fewer heuristics; training is slower per iteration (0.22 s against 0.13 s for VGGM) | The mechanism of S: forward on all, backward on the hardest only, with a real saving in the backward pass | Different domain and aim (detection accuracy, not FLOPs-to-target); no FLOP matching or tuned-baseline protocol |

### Novelty statement for the Improve track (draft v1, 2026-10-27; see D-025)

We do **not** claim: the rule of keeping the highest-loss rows of a minibatch (Ordered SGD, OHEM), skipping backward passes by loss (Selective-Backprop), a kept fraction that shrinks during training (Ordered SGD's default rule), or loss-based batch selection (Loshchilov and Hutter). What the project can claim today is the controlled, pre-registered measurement in `paper/CLAIMS.md` (C-21, C-27): S30 needs 35% to 38% fewer FLOPs than a tuned baseline on 20 paired seeds, not explained by batch size. A *method* claim needs a win over Selective-Backprop and over the Ordered-SGD schedule under the same FLOP accounting, which has not been tested. Whether prior work controlled for the tuning noise of the baseline is not known to us (not checked).
